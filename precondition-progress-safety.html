The Precondition of Progress: Why We Must Prove Safety Before We Scale

Published: March 3, 2026

In the rapidly shifting landscape of artificial intelligence, a fundamental rift has emerged, not in what we want to achieve, but in how we verify its integrity. As explored in the recent analysis of Sam Altman and Dario Amodei's divergent philosophies, the industry is currently split between a "Deployment" model—where safety is an emergent property discovered through trial and error—and a "Demonstration" model—where safety is a rigorous precondition that must be met before a system ever touches the public sphere.

While the "ship and iterate" model has defined the Silicon Valley success story for decades, I am writing today to argue that when it comes to Frontier AI, the Altman model is a category error. We are no longer building photo-sharing apps or social networks where the "failure mode" is a site outage or a UI bug. We are building systems with the potential for systemic, irreversible impact. Therefore, we must align ourselves with the theory that safety is not a feedback loop; it is a foundation.

The Fallacy of the Feedback Loop

The OpenAI philosophy, rooted in the Y Combinator tradition, suggests that the market is the ultimate truth-teller. The argument is that by releasing models incrementally, society "co-evolves" with the tech, and users identify the guardrails that developers missed. On the surface, this sounds democratic and pragmatic.

However, this theory relies on a dangerous assumption: that all failure modes are survivable. In software development, we are used to "hotfixes" and "patches." But as AI scales toward Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), we encounter risks that do not allow for a second chance. If a model provides a bad recipe for a chemical compound or identifies a vulnerability in a power grid to a bad actor, the "feedback" isn't a bug report—it's a catastrophe.

By relying on deployment as a discovery mechanism for safety, we are essentially using the public as an uncompensated, involuntary red team. We are treating the world as a laboratory, but in this lab, there are no walls and no way to recall the experiment once it has leaked.

Safety as a Precondition, Not a Feature

This is why the Anthropic "Demonstration" model—as championed by Dario Amodei—is the only intellectually honest path forward. Drawing from the worlds of physics and computational neuroscience, this theory treats AI safety with the same gravity we afford biosafety or aerospace engineering.

When we build a new aircraft, we do not put 300 people on it and "see if it stays up" to learn how to make it safer. We prove the physics. We simulate the stress. We demonstrate safety affirmatively through rigorous, internal protocols before the first passenger ever boards.

Anthropic's implementation of AI Safety Levels (ASL) is a vital step in this direction. By creating a framework where a model cannot be scaled until it is proven to be incapable of assisting in catastrophic misuse (such as the creation of biological weapons), we shift the burden of proof back to the creators. Safety ceases to be a feature added in version 2.0; it becomes the "license to operate" for version 1.0.

The Epistemological Divide: What Can We Prove?

The disagreement between Altman and Amodei is, at its heart, a question of what we can truly "know." Altman believes we cannot know a system is safe until it interacts with the infinite complexity of the real world. Amodei believes that if we cannot prove a system is safe through internal testing and formal verification, then it is, by definition, too dangerous to release.

I agree with the latter. If the developers of a model cannot articulate why a system is safe, or if they cannot demonstrate that it has internal guardrails that are robust against jailbreaking, then they have not finished the product. Releasing a "black box" and waiting to see what happens isn't innovation; it's an abdication of responsibility.

Why the Choice Matters Now

Refusing to choose a winner in this debate is itself a choice. It is a choice to let the most aggressive actor set the pace for the entire industry. If one company ships "fast and breaks things," every other company is pressured to do the same to remain competitive. This creates a race to the bottom where safety becomes a secondary concern to market share.

We must commit to the "Demonstration" theory because it is the only one that scales safely. As models become more autonomous and more integrated into our critical infrastructure, the cost of a "feedback loop" becomes too high to pay. We need a regulatory environment and an industry culture that rewards the "Amodei" approach—one that values the quiet, rigorous proof of safety over the loud, viral release of a new capability.

Conclusion: Reclaiming the Narrative

We have been conditioned by decades of "beta tests" and "early access" to think that unfinished, potentially buggy products are the norm. But AI is not "just another app." It is a foundational technology that requires a new set of principles.

To agree with the "Demonstration" theory of safety is to believe that the human creators of AI must remain the masters of its trajectory. It is to believe that "safe enough" isn't determined by an absence of complaints on a forum, but by an affirmative, scientific proof of robustness.

The choice is clear: Do we want to learn from our mistakes after they happen, or do we want to prevent them from happening in the first place? If we value the future of our society, we must choose the latter. Safety must be proven before it is scaled. Anything else is just a gamble we can't afford to lose.
